1 · Why this matters

AI is now part of the job search. Wise adoption is the goal.

Generative AI tools such as ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini now show up at every step of a civilian job search: résumés, cover letters, interview practice, and the screening systems on the employer's side. For a transitioning veteran, they promise help with the hardest part of the move, which is translating military experience into civilian terms. Research on veterans' transition treats that skill translation as a central question (Jasionowicz et al., 2026), and coaches in one program found that many veterans "had no idea how much experience they had in the military" (Porter et al., 2023, p. 30).

But AI adoption among veterans is uneven. Some use it daily. Others distrust it, and some have good reasons to: a tool that inflates a record or misstates a rank is an integrity problem, not a convenience. As a mentor, you are in the best position to help, because people decide about new tools mainly through trusted, person-to-person channels (Rogers, 2003). Your job is not to maximize adoption. It is to help your veteran adopt AI wisely: where it helps, with safeguards, and in their own voice.

Meet Alex, a composite veteran

Alex is a composite, not a real person: a Marine Corps logistics chief separating after twelve years, aiming for operations management. Alex is sharp, disciplined, and skeptical of anything that "makes stuff up." You will see Alex's words in the stage finder below.

2 · The model in brief

Diffusion of Innovations

Everett Rogers defined diffusion as "the process in which an innovation is communicated through certain channels over time among the members of a social system" (Rogers, 2003, p. 5). An innovation is anything perceived as new by the person deciding, so what counts is how AI looks to your veteran, not how good it actually is (Thompson, 2017).

The innovationGenerative AI, as your veteran perceives it.
Communication channelsAds and webinars build awareness. Trusted people, like you, change minds.
TimeEach person moves through decision stages at their own pace.
Social systemThe veteran's network: mentors, peers, family, and future employers.
Model at a glance
Focal levelIndividuals deciding within a social system, and the spread of those decisions across the system over time.
Core constructsFive perceived attributes, communication channels, time, the social system, five decision stages, and adopter categories.
MechanismHow people perceive an innovation's attributes, plus what they hear through trusted channels, shapes each person's decision. Individual decisions add up to an S-shaped curve of adoption.
Outcome explainedAdoption or rejection decisions, and the rate of adoption. Perceived attributes alone explain 49% to 87% of the variance in how fast innovations are adopted (Rogers, 1995, as cited in Ellsworth, 2000).

Key theorists and researchers

  • Everett M. Rogers built the theory across five editions of Diffusion of Innovations, from 1962 to 2003.
  • Bryce Ryan and Neal Gross (1943) studied how Iowa farmers adopted hybrid seed corn, the study that launched diffusion research.
  • Gary Moore and Izak Benbasat (1991) built a validated scale for measuring the perceived attributes, which made the theory testable for technology.
  • Penny Thompson (2017) wrote the open textbook chapter this resource remixes.
Why Diffusion, and not the other three models?

TAM predicts one person's intention from usefulness and ease of use, but says little about how influence travels between people. UTAUT adds social influence and support, but was built for workplaces deciding about a mandated system. CBAM tracks teachers implementing a program their school has already adopted. Mentoring is voluntary, person-to-person, and happens before and during the decision, which is the ground Diffusion was built for. It also names your role: Rogers calls people who informally sway others' decisions opinion leaders, and people who deliberately guide others through adoption change agents. A mentor can be both.

3 · Interactive · Stage finder

Where is your veteran right now?

Rogers describes five stages every person moves through when deciding about an innovation (Rogers, 2003; Thompson, 2017). Pick the stage that sounds like your veteran to see matching mentor moves. Use the arrow keys to move between stages.

Knowledge: learning that it exists

"I've heard of ChatGPT. I don't really know what it does."

What is going on

Alex knows AI exists but has no working picture of it. At this stage people gather basic information about what the innovation is and how it works.

Mentor moves

  • Show one concrete example tied to Alex's goal, such as turning one evaluation bullet into civilian language.
  • Explain plainly what AI is and is not: it predicts likely text, and it can be confidently wrong.
  • Keep it short. One example beats a lecture.

Ready for the next stage when: Alex asks how it could help with their own search.

Persuasion: forming an opinion

"My buddy said it made his résumé sound fake."

What is going on

Alex is forming a favorable or unfavorable attitude, and peers' stories carry real weight. A single bad story can settle the question unless someone trusted offers another view.

Mentor moves

  • Take the concern seriously. The buddy is right that unedited AI text can sound fake.
  • Show a weak example and a strong, edited one side by side.
  • Connect good use to values Alex already holds: accuracy, integrity, attention to detail.

Ready for the next stage when: Alex agrees to try it once, with you.

Decision: choosing to try it or not

"Okay. I'll try it, but just for my LinkedIn summary."

What is going on

Alex is choosing to adopt or reject. Rejection is a legitimate outcome, and a small, bounded trial is the healthiest way to decide.

Mentor moves

  • Agree on one low-stakes task.
  • Set ground rules first: no personal identifiers, and verify everything (see the checklist below).
  • Try it together in a session, so mistakes are caught on the spot.

Ready for the next stage when: Alex uses it on their own between sessions.

Implementation: putting it to work

"It wrote a decent bullet, but it called me a 'supply chain executive.'"

What is going on

Alex is using AI and judging its benefits. Problems surface here, and people adapt the tool to fit their needs, which Rogers calls re-invention.

Mentor moves

  • Teach the habit of checking every claim against Alex's own records and against My Next Move for Veterans.
  • Help Alex edit outputs back into their own voice.
  • Troubleshoot prompts together: ask for options, not one "final" answer.

Ready for the next stage when: Alex catches errors without your help.

Confirmation: keeping it, or not

"I used it to prep for three interviews. It's useful, but I check everything."

What is going on

Alex is looking for evidence that the decision was right. Good experiences confirm it; bad ones can reverse it. Dropping a tool that does not serve them is also a sound outcome.

Mentor moves

  • Reinforce the habits, not the tool: verification, privacy, ownership of the final words.
  • Fade your support on purpose. A scaffold should come down.
  • Invite Alex to show a peer how they use it, which spreads wise use through the network.

Signs of meaningful use: Alex uses AI selectively without prompting and can explain when not to use it.

4 · Interactive · Attribute explorer

Why is your veteran hesitating?

Rogers identified five perceived attributes that predict whether, and how fast, people adopt an innovation. In a 2024 study of students adopting ChatGPT, all five shaped adoption (Raman et al., 2024). Each one is a lever a mentor can pull.

Relative advantage: "Is it better than what I do now?"

Why they hesitate

"I can write my own résumé." If AI does not clearly beat the current way, there is no reason to change.

Mentor move

Show the payoff on a real task: three civilian versions of one military bullet in two minutes, for Alex to choose from and edit.

The evidence

Relative advantage is the most obvious attribute: whether the innovation is perceived as better than what it replaces (Rogers, 2003).

Compatibility: "Does it fit who I am?"

Why they hesitate

Military culture prizes accuracy and earned authority. A tool that inflates titles, or that does not understand military language, clashes with both.

Mentor move

Use a frame that fits: AI has not earned its stripes. It can sound like an expert without the proven knowledge behind the rank, so treat it like a new troop whose work gets checked.

The evidence

Veterans describe the military as having "our own language" that outsiders may not understand (Porter et al., 2023, p. 30). Compatibility weighed heavily for some ChatGPT adopters (Raman et al., 2024).

Complexity: "Is it hard to use?"

Why they hesitate

"I don't know what to type." A blank prompt box feels like a test.

Mentor move

Hand over two or three starter prompts and one task at a time. Example: "Here is a bullet from my military evaluation. Suggest three civilian versions. Do not add anything I did not do."

The evidence

Generative AI literacy builds in a progression, from a basic understanding of AI toward skills such as prompting, alongside ethical and legal awareness (Annapureddy et al., 2024). Start at the beginning.

Trialability: "Can I try it safely?"

Why they hesitate

Fear of a costly mistake, and of where their information goes.

Mentor move

Try it in session with made-up or non-sensitive details. Nothing gets submitted, and Alex can walk away at any point.

The evidence

Innovations people can try a little at a time spread faster, and trialability matters most to the earliest adopters, who have no one to watch yet (Rogers, 1995, as cited in Ellsworth, 2000).

Observability: "Have I seen it work for someone like me?"

Why they hesitate

Alex has seen AI hype, but never a veteran using it well.

Mentor move

Show a before-and-after example, and let Alex watch you use AI in your own work, including the moment you catch and fix a mistake.

The evidence

Seeing others use an innovation successfully increases adoption (Thompson, 2017), and person-to-person channels outweigh mass media (Rogers, 2003).

5 · Interactive · Adopter curve

Not every veteran adopts at the same speed

Rogers sorted adopters into five groups by when they adopt. Once a critical mass adopts, spread becomes self-sustaining (Rogers, 2003). Select a group for a one-line mentoring tip.

Select a group to see a tip.

Why "cautious adopters"? Rogers' label for the last group is "laggards." Thompson (2017) notes the label is judgmental and oversimplified. A veteran who refuses to paste their record into a chatbot may be showing sound privacy and security judgment, so this guide uses a neutral name.

7 · Applications and implications

Putting the model to work

Instructional application

Treat each mentoring session as a short learning design. Find the stage, choose one matched move, model the skill, practice it together, and step back as the veteran gains confidence.

Organizational application

A mentoring program can use Diffusion to spread sound AI guidance across its whole network: identify mentors who are early adopters to act as opinion leaders, share real examples so good use is visible, offer safe practice materials, and watch how quickly guidance spreads across different mentor groups.

Implications
ForWhat Diffusion suggests
AdoptionAim for wise adoption, not maximum adoption. Rogers warns of a pro-innovation bias, and a veteran's informed "no" is a valid outcome.
ImplementationExpect re-invention. Veterans will adapt AI to their own needs, so set guardrails (the checklist) rather than a script.
ResearchMost studies of AI adoption through Diffusion sample students (Raman et al., 2024). How mentors shape veterans' AI adoption, and whether it improves transition outcomes, is an open question.
PracticeMentors are the trusted channel that changes minds. Their own visible, careful use is the most persuasive demonstration available.
What Diffusion can diagnose
  • Where a veteran is in deciding
  • Which perceived attribute is the barrier
  • Who influences whom in the network
What it cannot diagnose
  • Whether AI actually improves the job search
  • How well the veteran uses it once adopted
  • Barriers outside perception, like access or cost

8 · Annotated resources

Go further

My Next Move for Veterans (National Center for O*NET Development, U.S. Department of Labor). An official, free tool that matches a military job code to related civilian careers. Mentors can use it to check whether an AI's skill translation holds up.

Generative AI Profile, NIST AI 600-1 (Autio et al., 2024). The federal government's guide to the main risks of generative AI, including confabulation, data privacy, and human over-reliance on AI. Useful background for the checklist.

Diffusion of Innovation, in Foundations of Educational Technology (Thompson, 2017). The open textbook section this resource remixes, with two short videos on the theory. A quick, plain-language deeper read.

10 · About this resource

Design rationale, AI use, and license

Design rationale: UDL and learning theory

Universal Design for Learning, for this audience

UDL is not optional here. In 2025, 34% of U.S. veterans, and 50% of veterans who served since September 2001, reported a service-connected disability (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2026). Some mentors are veterans themselves, and the guidance mentors pass on should model access. Design choices follow the UDL Guidelines 3.0 (CAST, 2024):

  • Engagement. Mentors start at their veteran's current stage instead of reading top to bottom. Examples draw on military values, and the neutral term "cautious adopters" avoids a judgmental label.
  • Representation. Every concept appears as text plus a visual or worked example. The adopter curve has a full text description, jargon is defined in plain language, and the page adapts to light or dark mode and to text resizing.
  • Action and expression. Mentors act on the content through the stage finder and checklist. Every control works by keyboard, targets are at least 44 pixels tall, nothing is timed, and nothing is collected.

Learning theory: the More Knowledgeable Other

Vygotsky (1978) describes learning as moving forward with help from a More Knowledgeable Other, and Wood, Bruner, and Ross (1976) describe the help as scaffolding that is withdrawn as skill grows. The military already runs on this idea: noncommissioned officers earn rank through experience and proven knowledge, and junior troops learn from them. That makes mentoring compatible with military culture, in Rogers' terms. The stage finder places the veteran, the mentor moves are the scaffolds, and the confirmation stage ends by fading support. It also explains the core caution: AI can sound like a More Knowledgeable Other without having earned the rank, so the human mentor stays in the loop.

Learning theory: multimedia design

Mayer's (2009) principles shaped the page itself: content is split into short, learner-paced segments (segmenting), headings and section numbers cue the structure (signaling), and decorative material is left out (coherence).

Evidence

The theory comes from Rogers (2003), Thompson (2017), and Ellsworth (2000), a Module 5 course reading. Recent evidence comes from Raman et al. (2024) on AI adoption, Porter et al. (2023) and Jasionowicz et al. (2026) on veterans' transition, and Annapureddy et al. (2024) and Autio et al. (2024) on responsible AI use.
